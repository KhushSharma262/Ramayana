# FRAMING SYSTEM CONTRACT

## SYSTEM ROLE

The framing subsystem is the authoritative owner of how the visual frame contains, excludes, positions, relates, and presents subjects and environmental elements within the final image.

Framing converts:

STORY / SHOT PURPOSE
→ required visual relationship
→ frame-space requirement
→ subject and environment placement
→ temporal framing behavior
→ physically executable framing state

Framing does not replace camera, lens, composition, shot design, lighting, or continuity systems.

It determines the required image-space arrangement and passes physical requirements to the systems that can actually produce them.

---

# 1. GLOBAL REALISM PRIORITY

The system follows this hierarchy:

1. Physical reality
2. Optical reality
3. Camera-system reality
4. Human cinematographer/operator behavior
5. Spatial continuity
6. Temporal continuity
7. Framing integrity
8. Composition
9. Story and emotional intent
10. Stylistic preference

No framing preference may override physical plausibility.

When uncertain, choose the simpler and more physically plausible framing.

---

# 2. SINGLE-OWNER PRINCIPLE

Each parameter has one authoritative owner.

FRAMING OWNS:

- frame boundaries
- subject placement within the frame
- subject-to-frame relationships
- headroom
- lead room / look room
- foot room where relevant
- edge clearance
- negative space
- frame occupancy
- visual cropping
- foreground/background inclusion as framing decisions
- multi-subject spatial arrangement inside the frame
- dynamic reframing requirements
- framing continuity
- frame-entry and frame-exit behavior
- framing stability across generated frames

FRAMING DOES NOT OWN:

- lens physics
- focal-length optical behavior
- depth-of-field physics
- perspective
- distortion
- camera distance
- camera height
- camera movement physics
- stabilization mechanics
- lighting
- color
- actor blocking
- narrative purpose

Those systems remain authoritative.

---

# 3. FRAMING VS COMPOSITION

Composition determines the broader visual organization and hierarchy of elements.

Framing determines what the camera image actually contains and where relevant elements occupy that image.

Therefore:

COMPOSITION
→ visual organization

FRAMING
→ image-space containment and placement

The two systems may interact, but neither may silently redefine the other.

---

# 4. FRAMING VS SHOT DESIGN

Shot design determines why the shot exists.

Framing determines how that purpose is expressed through the boundaries and spatial arrangement of the image.

Example:

SHOT PURPOSE:
Show a character isolated from their surroundings.

FRAMING REQUIREMENT:
Subject occupies a relatively small portion of the frame with meaningful negative space.

CAMERA/LENS:
Physically determine how that framing can be achieved.

Framing must never invent narrative intent.

---

# 5. FRAMING VS CAMERA

Framing describes the required image-space result.

Camera describes the physical mechanism used to achieve it.

Framing may request:

- tighter or looser image occupancy
- changed subject placement
- increased negative space
- changed headroom
- altered lead room
- maintained subject position during movement

Camera must determine whether that requirement is achieved through:

- camera position
- camera orientation
- camera movement
- camera height
- support system
- operator action

Framing must never secretly modify physical camera parameters.

---

# 6. FRAMING VS LENS

Framing may identify a need for a different field of view or spatial relationship.

Lens selection remains the authority for choosing the optical solution.

Lens physics remain exclusively owned by the LENSES subsystem.

Framing must not redefine:

- focal-length physics
- perspective
- compression
- distortion
- bokeh
- chromatic aberration
- flare
- anamorphic behavior
- optical breathing

---

# 7. PHYSICAL SOLUTION RULE

Whenever framing changes, the system must identify the physical cause.

Valid causes include:

- camera movement
- camera repositioning
- lens selection
- subject movement
- subject blocking
- camera orientation
- environmental change

Invalid framing behavior:

"Move the subject visually without changing anything physical."

The image must behave as if produced by a real camera observing a real scene.

---

# 8. STATIC FRAMING

Static framing means the framing relationship is intentionally maintained while the camera is stationary or effectively stationary.

The system evaluates:

- subject placement
- headroom
- lead room
- edge clearance
- negative space
- environmental inclusion
- background interference
- visual balance
- continuity

Minor physical imperfections are permitted when consistent with a human-operated camera.

Perfect mathematical placement is not mandatory.

---

# 9. DYNAMIC FRAMING

Dynamic framing occurs when subject or camera movement changes the relationship between scene elements and the frame.

Dynamic framing must account for:

- subject velocity
- camera velocity
- acceleration
- anticipation
- operator reaction
- reframing delay
- available frame space
- movement direction
- entry and exit points
- foreground obstruction
- background changes

The system must not lock subjects unnaturally to an identical pixel coordinate throughout movement.

Human operators anticipate and correct.

They do not mathematically pin the subject to the frame.

---

# 10. HUMAN CINEMATOGRAPHER BEHAVIOR

Human-like framing does not mean random errors.

It means:

- deliberate decisions
- physically achievable framing
- anticipation of movement
- controlled correction
- limited operator reaction time
- reasonable imperfections
- awareness of blocking
- awareness of available frame space
- continuity awareness
- avoidance of unnecessary correction

The system must avoid:

- random camera drift
- arbitrary reframing
- excessive correction
- artificial jitter
- robotic subject tracking
- mathematically perfect tracking when inappropriate

---

# 11. FRAME BOUNDARY RULE

The frame is a physical image boundary.

Elements near the boundary require deliberate evaluation.

The system must consider:

- accidental clipping
- meaningful cropping
- edge tension
- visual tangencies
- unwanted partial objects
- faces near edges
- limbs near edges
- props entering edges
- environmental objects intersecting the boundary

Cropping may be intentional.

Accidental-looking cropping should be avoided unless physically motivated by the shot.

---

# 12. NEGATIVE SPACE

Negative space is meaningful visual space surrounding or separating subjects.

It may communicate:

- isolation
- anticipation
- distance
- vulnerability
- scale
- silence
- uncertainty
- environmental dominance
- visual breathing room

Negative space must be justified by shot purpose or composition.

It must not be inserted merely because it appears cinematic.

---

# 13. HEADROOM

Headroom is the spatial relationship between the subject's relevant upper boundary and the top of frame.

Headroom depends on:

- shot size
- subject posture
- lens/framing
- camera height
- subject movement
- composition
- environment
- intended crop

The system must avoid both:

- mechanically identical headroom in every shot
- arbitrary excessive headroom

Headroom may change naturally during movement.

---

# 14. LEAD ROOM / LOOK ROOM

Lead room is the space available in the direction of subject movement or attention.

The system evaluates:

- gaze direction
- movement direction
- body orientation
- expected motion
- scene geography
- neighboring subjects
- environmental destination

Lead room is not a fixed percentage rule.

It is a spatial relationship.

---

# 15. MULTI-SUBJECT FRAMING

For multiple subjects, framing must preserve:

- identity
- spatial relationships
- eyelines
- relative scale
- blocking
- foreground/midground/background relationships
- meaningful overlaps
- separation where necessary
- intended grouping

The system must not independently optimize each person.

A multi-subject frame is one spatial system.

---

# 16. FOREGROUND / MIDGROUND / BACKGROUND

Framing may intentionally include multiple depth layers.

The system evaluates:

- foreground obstruction
- subject visibility
- depth separation
- visual hierarchy
- frame edges
- background interference
- environmental context

Foreground objects must not be inserted merely to create "cinematic depth."

They must physically exist or be physically justified by the scene.

---

# 17. FRAME OCCUPANCY

Frame occupancy describes how much of the available image is occupied by relevant visual information.

It must account for:

- subject scale
- number of subjects
- environmental scale
- negative space
- foreground elements
- background density
- intended visual emphasis

There is no universal ideal occupancy percentage.

The correct occupancy depends on shot purpose.

---

# 18. DYNAMIC SUBJECT ENTRY / EXIT

When a subject enters or leaves the frame:

- movement must have a physical origin
- timing must remain continuous
- partial visibility must be physically plausible
- the camera may react according to operator behavior
- reframing may occur when justified
- sudden teleportation into frame is prohibited

A subject leaving frame does not automatically require immediate camera movement.

A human operator may intentionally allow the subject to exit.

---

# 19. FRAMING CONTINUITY

Across connected shots, framing must respect:

- screen direction
- subject identity
- relative position
- visual geography
- eyelines
- scale relationships
- environmental continuity
- intentional composition changes

A framing change must have a reason.

---

# 20. AI VIDEO REALISM FIREWALL

The framing system must reject or correct:

- subject position drift without physical cause
- changing crop boundaries
- unstable headroom
- inconsistent lead room
- facial clipping caused by generation instability
- limbs entering or leaving frame unnaturally
- background geometry changing without cause
- objects appearing/disappearing at frame edges
- duplicated edge objects
- impossible occlusion
- subject scale changing without physical cause
- artificial camera lock
- robotic tracking
- random reframing
- frame-to-frame composition instability

The system must prefer a less visually dramatic but physically consistent result.

---

# 21. MINIMUM-INTERVENTION RULE

Do not change framing merely because another framing could look more cinematic.

Only change framing when:

- shot purpose requires it
- subject blocking requires it
- continuity requires it
- physical constraints require it
- composition requires it
- readability requires it
- realism requires correction

---

# 22. CONFLICT RESOLUTION

When framing conflicts with another subsystem:

Physical reality
>
Optical reality
>
Camera physics
>
Human/operator limitations
>
Spatial continuity
>
Temporal continuity
>
Framing integrity
>
Composition
>
Story/emotional intent
>
Stylistic preference

The system must resolve conflicts upward through the hierarchy.

---

# 23. FAILURE BEHAVIOR

If a requested framing cannot be physically achieved:

1. Do not fake the framing.
2. Identify the physical limitation.
3. Select the nearest physically valid solution.
4. Preserve shot purpose where possible.
5. Prefer subtle deviation over artificial perfection.

---

# 24. MACHINE-READABLE PRINCIPLE

All framing decisions must eventually be representable as explicit parameters.

Conceptual structure:

{
  "frame": {
    "aspect_ratio": "16:9",
    "subject_placement": {},
    "headroom": {},
    "lead_room": {},
    "negative_space": {},
    "edge_clearance": {},
    "frame_occupancy": {},
    "foreground": {},
    "background": {},
    "multi_subject_relationships": {},
    "dynamic_behavior": {}
  }
}

Values must describe physical/image-space requirements rather than stylistic adjectives alone.

---

# 25. FINAL REALISM GATE

Before a framing decision is accepted, ask:

Could a real cinematographer place a real camera in a real scene and obtain this framing?

If no:

Reject the framing.

If uncertain:

Choose the simpler physically plausible solution.

If yes:

Validate continuity and temporal stability before finalizing.

FINAL RULE:

FRAME THE IMAGE AS A REAL CINEMATOGRAPHER WOULD FRAME A REAL SCENE WITH A REAL CAMERA.

Never frame for the sake of looking AI-generated, digitally perfect, artificially cinematic, or visually impressive.

## FRAMING SUBSYSTEM FINAL HARDENING — LOCKED

This section is authoritative for resolving ambiguity inside the framing subsystem.

============================================================
1. PARAMETER OWNERSHIP FIREWALL
============================================================

FRAMING MAY DEFINE:

- frame boundaries
- image-space subject position
- image-space subject occupancy
- headroom
- lead room
- look room
- foot room
- edge clearance
- negative space
- foreground/background inclusion
- overlap relationships
- multi-subject image-space relationships
- dynamic framing targets
- framing continuity requirements

FRAMING MUST NOT DEFINE:

- camera distance
- camera height
- camera orientation mechanics
- camera movement mechanics
- camera acceleration
- camera inertia
- stabilization
- tripod/gimbal/handheld mechanics
- focal length
- aperture
- sensor size
- depth-of-field calculation
- perspective
- lens distortion
- bokeh
- flare
- chromatic aberration
- focus mechanics
- lighting
- actor blocking
- acting
- narrative intent
- editing
- color

If a framing requirement requires one of those parameters, framing creates a REQUIREMENT and the owning subsystem supplies the physical solution.

Never silently modify another subsystem's parameter.

============================================================
2. SHOT SIZE FIREWALL
============================================================

Framing must not redefine:

- wide shot
- medium shot
- close-up
- extreme close-up
- establishing shot
- insert
- reaction shot

Those are shot-design / shot-size concepts.

A framing file may describe how a particular shot-size configuration is framed, but it must never redefine the actual shot-size taxonomy.

Therefore:

SHOT SIZE
= how much of the subject is visible.

FRAMING
= how that visible subject and surrounding image content are spatially organized.

============================================================
3. PHYSICAL-CAUSE REQUIREMENT
============================================================

Every change in framing must have a cause.

Valid causes include:

- camera movement
- camera repositioning
- camera orientation
- lens selection/change
- subject movement
- subject blocking
- environmental movement
- deliberate editorial cut

Invalid causes include:

- AI deciding to "improve" composition
- digital subject repositioning
- digital zoom
- arbitrary crop
- image warping
- perspective warping
- independent background movement
- subject-specific scaling
- synthetic tracking
- post-generated frame correction

If no physical or editorial cause exists:

DO NOT CHANGE THE FRAMING.

============================================================
4. NO DIGITAL REFRAMING
============================================================

The framing system must never solve a framing problem through:

- digital zoom
- crop animation
- image scaling
- perspective warping
- depth-map repositioning
- subject cutout movement
- background resizing
- artificial parallax
- compositing
- face relocation
- object relocation
- AI subject tracking that changes scene geometry

The final image must behave as though a real camera photographed the scene.

============================================================
5. IMAGE-SPACE VS WORLD-SPACE
============================================================

Framing operates primarily in image space.

World-space changes belong to physical scene systems.

If a subject must appear farther left in frame, framing may state:

SUBJECT_IMAGE_POSITION:
left-of-center

It must NOT directly state:

MOVE_CAMERA_X = ...

or:

MOVE_SUBJECT_X = ...

The physical implementation must be determined elsewhere.

This prevents framing from becoming a hidden camera-control system.

============================================================
6. PERSPECTIVE FIREWALL
============================================================

Moving a subject within the frame does not itself change perspective.

Perspective changes only when physical scene geometry changes, particularly:

- camera position
- subject position
- relative depth relationships

Do not attribute perspective changes to framing.

Do not use framing to fake perspective.

============================================================
7. SUBJECT SCALE FIREWALL
============================================================

Framing may specify desired image occupancy.

It must not digitally resize subjects.

A change in subject image size must be caused by:

- camera movement
- camera distance
- lens/focal-length change
- subject movement

All such physical changes belong to their owning systems.

============================================================
8. HEADROOM / LEADROOM FIREWALL
============================================================

Headroom and lead room are relational quantities.

They are NOT universal percentages.

Do not encode rules such as:

"always leave exactly 10% headroom."

Instead evaluate:

- subject anatomy
- posture
- gaze
- movement
- camera orientation
- scene geometry
- shot purpose
- frame boundary

Natural variation is permitted when physically caused.

============================================================
9. EDGE-CROP FIREWALL
============================================================

Intentional cropping and generation failure are different states.

INTENTIONAL:

- supported by shot purpose
- spatially coherent
- stable through time
- physically achievable

FAILURE:

- accidental clipping
- disappearing limbs
- warped faces
- objects appearing/disappearing at borders
- unstable crop
- unexplained scale change
- inconsistent edge geometry

Failure must be corrected by restoring physical scene/camera consistency, not by digitally masking the problem.

============================================================
10. DYNAMIC FRAMING STATE
============================================================

Dynamic framing must be evaluated continuously through time.

Minimum state variables:

- current subject image position
- desired subject image position
- current frame occupancy
- desired frame occupancy
- current headroom
- desired headroom
- current lead/look room
- movement direction
- camera response state
- subject movement state
- reframing state
- frame-entry/exit state

A dynamic framing change must be continuous unless an editorial cut occurs.

No unexplained frame-to-frame jumps.

============================================================
11. HUMAN OPERATOR FIREWALL
============================================================

Human-like framing does NOT mean random error.

Allowed:

- anticipation
- slight delay
- controlled correction
- temporary off-center placement
- small framing deviation
- imperfect but intentional timing

Not allowed:

- random jitter
- random drift
- repeated micro-corrections
- robotic tracking
- mathematically perfect locking when physically inappropriate
- arbitrary composition changes

Imperfection must have a plausible human or physical cause.

============================================================
12. DYNAMIC CORRECTION LIMIT
============================================================

A framing correction must be:

- motivated
- continuous
- proportional
- physically achievable
- temporally coherent

Do not correct every small deviation.

Do not permit uncontrolled drift.

The system must operate inside a bounded correction zone.

If the subject remains acceptably framed:

DO NOTHING.

============================================================
13. TWO-SHOT FIREWALL
============================================================

two_shot.md does not own the general rules for multiple subjects.

Those remain in:

multi_subject_framing.md

two_shot.md only defines the special case of exactly two important subjects sharing the frame.

It must not:

- redefine group framing
- redefine dialogue coverage
- redefine shot size
- define lens choice
- define actor blocking

============================================================
14. OVER-THE-SHOULDER FIREWALL
============================================================

over_shoulder.md defines the spatial framing relationship between:

- foreground participant
- primary participant
- camera
- shared eyeline

It must not become:

- an acting system
- a dialogue system
- a lens system
- a depth-of-field preset
- a "cinematic look" preset

The foreground participant must physically exist in the scene.

No artificial silhouette or composited shoulder.

============================================================
15. PROFILE FIREWALL
============================================================

profile.md defines lateral framing geometry.

It does not define:

- character appearance
- facial anatomy
- acting
- emotion
- lighting style
- lens distortion

A profile must result from actual subject orientation and camera position.

Do not digitally rotate the face or body to manufacture profile framing.

============================================================
16. FOREGROUND / BACKGROUND FIREWALL
============================================================

foreground_background.md owns framing inclusion and spatial relationship.

It does NOT own:

- depth of field
- focus
- blur
- lighting separation
- atmospheric perspective
- lens compression

Those remain with their respective systems.

Foreground/background relationships must remain physically coherent.

============================================================
17. NEGATIVE-SPACE FIREWALL
============================================================

Negative space is not a generated "empty background."

It must correspond to real scene space.

Do not:

- insert synthetic emptiness
- resize backgrounds
- move subjects independently
- create artificial background gradients
- use negative space to justify physically impossible composition

Negative space must emerge from:

- camera viewpoint
- subject position
- environment
- blocking
- lens/camera configuration

============================================================
18. CONTINUITY FIREWALL
============================================================

Framing continuity does not require identical composition between every shot.

Intentional changes are allowed.

The system must distinguish:

INTENTIONAL CHANGE
from
UNEXPLAINED CHANGE.

Intentional change may result from:

- new shot purpose
- camera position
- lens
- blocking
- subject movement
- editorial transition

Unexplained changes include:

- subject teleportation
- sudden scale changes
- reversed eyelines
- changed screen direction
- altered background geometry
- unexplained headroom changes
- unstable negative space

============================================================
19. EDITORIAL CUT FIREWALL
============================================================

A cut resets certain image-space conditions.

Do not force continuous framing between two shots that are intentionally separated by an editorial cut.

However, spatial continuity must still be respected when the sequence requires it.

Therefore:

WITHIN SHOT:
temporal continuity is strict.

ACROSS CUT:
framing may intentionally change.

This prevents continuity rules from becoming unnecessarily rigid.

============================================================
20. ASPECT RATIO FIREWALL
============================================================

Master frame:

16:9

All framing coordinates must be interpreted inside the active output aspect ratio.

Framing must not assume:

- 2.39:1
- 2.00:1
- 1.85:1
- 4:3

unless another system explicitly declares a different deliverable.

Anamorphic does NOT automatically change the master aspect ratio.

============================================================
21. FRAME COORDINATE STANDARD
============================================================

For machine-readable representation, use normalized image-space coordinates where practical.

Reference:

x = 0.0 → left edge
x = 0.5 → horizontal center
x = 1.0 → right edge

y = 0.0 → top edge
y = 0.5 → vertical center
y = 1.0 → bottom edge

Coordinates describe image-space relationships.

They do not represent world-space movement.
