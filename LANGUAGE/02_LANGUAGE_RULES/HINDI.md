## 1. PURPOSE

This file defines the Hindi used for:

- primary spoken dialogue
- adapted dialogue
- connective dialogue
- screenplay narration where Hindi is appropriate
- performance-oriented translations
- character language profiles
- Hindi-language subtitles
- Hindi-language production instructions

It does NOT define:

- original Awadhi grammar
- Sanskrit grammar
- Sanskrit pronunciation
- voice identity
- acting
- audio mixing

Those belong to their respective systems.

---

# 2. HINDI STANDARD FOR THIS PROJECT

The production standard is:

REFINED
+
NATURAL
+
DIGNIFIED
+
GRAMMATICALLY CORRECT
+
PERFORMABLE
+
CULTURALLY APPROPRIATE
+
ACCESSIBLE

The project uses a form of Hindi suitable for serious historical/mythological drama.

It is NOT:

- contemporary social-media Hindi
- Gen-Z Hindi
- internet Hindi
- slang-heavy Hindi
- casual Bollywood Hindi
- artificial Sanskritized Hindi
- artificially archaic Hindi
- machine-translated Hindi
- bureaucratic Hindi
- textbook-only Hindi

---

# 3. SPOKEN FORMAL HINDI

The project should generally target natural spoken formal Hindi rather than extremely Sanskritized written Hindi.

This distinction is important.

A sentence may be grammatically correct in highly formal written Hindi but still sound unnatural when spoken by an actor.

Preferred:

natural spoken form
+
grammatical correctness
+
dignity

Avoid:

frozen/formal literary construction
when a simpler natural construction expresses the same meaning.

Hindi linguistic research distinguishes spoken formal Hindi from a more heavily Sanskritized frozen formal variety. The project should generally follow the former unless source, ritual, character, or context requires the latter.

---

# 4. SENTENCE STRUCTURE

Hindi is generally a head-final language.

Default neutral order:

SUBJECT
+
OBJECT / COMPLEMENTS
+
VERB

Example:

राम वन गए।

Do not force English-style sentence order into Hindi.

Preferred:

सीता ने राम को देखा।

Not:

सीता राम को देखी ने।

Natural variation is permitted when motivated by:

- emphasis
- topic
- contrast
- emotion
- poetic structure
- dialogue rhythm
- question formation
- source preservation

---

# 5. WORD ORDER

Neutral order should be used unless there is a reason to alter it.

Possible motivations for altered order:

- emphasis
- contrast
- emotional reaction
- dramatic reveal
- topicalization
- poetic effect
- source-derived construction

Do not rearrange words merely to imitate English syntax.

---

# 6. POSTPOSITIONS

Hindi primarily uses postpositions rather than English-style prepositions.

Common examples:

में
से
को
पर
तक
के लिए
के साथ
के बिना
के कारण
के पास
के सामने
के भीतर
के बाहर
के ऊपर
के नीचे

Examples:

वन में

राम के साथ

राजा के सामने

तुम्हारे लिए

Use the appropriate oblique form before postpositions.

---

# 7. OBLIQUE FORMS

Nouns and pronouns may change form when followed by postpositions.

Examples:

लड़का → लड़के को

राजा → राजा को

राम → राम को

मैं → मुझसे

तुम → तुमसे

वह → उससे

यह → इससे

Do not mechanically apply one ending to every noun.

Nominal gender, number, lexical form, and declension must be checked individually.

---

# 8. GENDER

Hindi has grammatical gender:

- masculine
- feminine

Gender affects:

- adjectives
- participles
- many verb forms
- agreement
- some pronoun forms

Examples:

सुंदर बालक

सुंदर बालिका

बालक गया।

बालिका गई।

Gender must follow the grammatical gender of the noun, not the biological assumptions of the writer.

---

# 9. NUMBER

Hindi distinguishes:

- singular
- plural

Plurality may be expressed through:

- noun morphology
- pronouns
- verb agreement
- quantifiers
- context

Examples:

लड़का गया।

लड़के गए।

स्त्री आई।

स्त्रियाँ आईं।

Do not add plural markers where the noun is already grammatically plural.

---

# 10. ADJECTIVE AGREEMENT

Many Hindi adjectives agree with the noun in gender and number.

Example:

अच्छा राजा

अच्छी रानी

अच्छे राजा

अच्छी रानियाँ

However, indeclinable adjectives do not change.

Example:

सुंदर राजा

सुंदर रानी

सुंदर बालक

सुंदर बालिकाएँ

Do not force agreement onto indeclinable adjectives.

तुम्हारे

उसका

Example:

मेरे भाई

मेरी बहनें

Not the gender of the possessor.

Core Hindi pronouns:

हम

SECOND PERSON:

आप

THIRD PERSON:

Pronoun selection must be controlled by relationship and context.

---

# 13. SECOND-PERSON REGISTER

The three major second-person forms have different pragmatic values.

तू:

- highly intimate
- highly familiar
- potentially affectionate
- potentially insulting
- potentially devotional/traditional depending on context

It must NOT be inserted simply because a character is emotionally close.

तुम:

- familiar
- less formal than आप
- appropriate in selected intimate, familial, peer, or familiar relationships

आप:

- respectful
- formal
- honorific
- socially appropriate toward many elders, authorities, respected persons, or deities depending on context

IMPORTANT:

Pronoun choice is relationship-specific.

Do not create a universal rule such as:

"all gods = आप"

or

"all husbands and wives = तुम."

The exact form must be determined from character relationship, textual evidence, tradition, and project decision.

---

# 14. PRONOUN CONSISTENCY

Once a relationship has an approved pronoun pattern, do not randomly switch.

A switch requires a reason such as:

- emotional escalation
- intimacy
- anger
- humiliation
- reconciliation
- change in social status
- deliberate rhetorical effect
- source quotation

Any major recurring relationship should have its own entry in:

03_EPISODES/EPISODE_XX/RELATIONSHIPS/

---

# 15. HONORIFICITY

Respect may be expressed through:

- आप
- titles
- names
- kinship terms
- honorific nouns
- respectful verb forms
- indirect phrasing
- blessings
- deferential constructions

Respect should not be communicated by inserting "जी" everywhere.

Example:

राजन्

महाराज

प्रभु

भगवन्

माता

पिता

गुरुदेव

are context-dependent forms and must be source-supported or approved.

---

# 16. HONORIFIC "जी"

"जी" is a Hindi honorific particle.

It may occur after names or address terms where natural and appropriate.

However:

DO NOT mechanically append जी to every character name.

Avoid:

राम जी जी

भगवान जी महाराज जी

unless there is a genuine contextual reason.

For major sacred characters, preferred address forms should be established separately through source and relationship research.

---

# 17. VERB AGREEMENT

Hindi verbs may agree with:

- subject
- object
- gender
- number
- person

depending on construction.

Agreement must therefore be evaluated at the sentence level.

Do not assume that the nearest noun always determines agreement.

---

# 18. PERFECTIVE / ERGATIVE CONSTRUCTIONS

Hindi has an ergative pattern in many perfective transitive constructions.

The agent may take:

ने

Example:

राम ने रावण को मारा।

सीता ने फल खाया।

In such constructions, agreement behavior differs from ordinary present/intransitive constructions.

This must be treated as grammar, not as optional stylistic variation.

Do not remove ने merely because an English translation does not contain an equivalent word.

---

# 19. PERFECTIVE AGREEMENT

Perfective transitive constructions require special attention to agreement.

Examples:

राम ने सीता को देखा।

सीता ने राम को देखा।

Where the direct object is unmarked, agreement may occur with the object.

Where the object is marked with को, the verb commonly takes a default masculine singular form.

The exact construction must be checked rather than generated mechanically.

---

# 20. TENSE AND ASPECT

Hindi verbal meaning is strongly organized through aspectual constructions.

Important distinctions include:

- habitual
- progressive
- perfective
- future
- modal/irrealis constructions

Common auxiliaries include:

है
हूँ
हो
हैं
था
थी
थे
होगा
होगी
होंगे

Aspectual markers include:

ता / ती / ते

रहा / रही / रहे

आ / ई / ए

and related constructions.

Do not treat Hindi tense as a simple one-to-one equivalent of English tense.

---

# 21. HABITUAL

Habitual constructions commonly use:

-ता
-ती
-ते

with the appropriate auxiliary or contextual construction.

Examples:

वह प्रतिदिन आता है।

वह प्रायः प्रार्थना करती है।

Use habitual aspect when describing:

- repeated action
- customary action
- characteristic behavior
- general tendency

---

# 22. PROGRESSIVE

Progressive constructions commonly use:

रहा
रही
रहे

Examples:

वह जा रहा है।

वह प्रतीक्षा कर रही है।

वे बात कर रहे हैं।

Use progressive aspect for an action understood as ongoing.

Do not insert progressive constructions simply because an English source uses "is/was."

---

# 23. PERFECTIVE

Perfective constructions commonly use forms such as:

किया
गया
आया
देखा
लिखा
कहा

The construction must be interpreted according to:

- transitivity
- aspect
- gender
- number
- agreement
- auxiliary

---

# 24. FUTURE

Hindi future forms express:

- future action
- intention
- prediction
- expectation
- sometimes command-like force depending on context

Examples:

मैं जाऊँगा।

वह आएगी।

वे करेंगे।

Future forms should reflect character, gender, number, person, and context.

---

# 25. NEGATION

The primary Hindi negative particle is:

नहीं

Examples:

मैं नहीं जाऊँगा।

वह नहीं आई।

हम ऐसा नहीं करेंगे।

Negation must normally occur in the appropriate position before the verbal predicate.

Do not imitate English negative placement.

---

# 26. EXISTENTIAL / COPULAR NEGATION

Use:

नहीं है
नहीं था
नहीं होगा

where grammatically appropriate.

Do not replace all negative copular constructions with artificial literary alternatives.

---

# 27. IMPERATIVES

Hindi commands vary by social relationship.

Broadly:

तू:
  very intimate / familiar / potentially harsh

तुम:
  familiar command

आप:
  respectful command/request

Examples:

बैठ।

बैठो।

बैठिए।

The exact choice must follow relationship.

---

# 28. POLITE REQUESTS

Politeness may be expressed through:

- कृपया
- imperative forms
- conditional phrasing
- indirect requests
- respectful address
- modal constructions

Do not put कृपया before every request.

Natural dialogue may use:

कृपया बैठिए।

but may also use:

बैठिए।

or a contextually appropriate indirect form.

---

# 29. QUESTIONS

Hindi questions may use:

क्या

कौन

किसे

किसका

किससे

कहाँ

कब

क्यों

कैसे

कितना

कितनी

कितने

Question syntax should remain natural Hindi.

Examples:

आप कहाँ जा रहे हैं?

यह किसका धनुष है?

तुम यहाँ क्यों आए?

Do not mechanically translate English question structures.

---

# 30. RELATIVE-CORRELATIVE STRUCTURES

Hindi commonly uses paired structures such as:

जो ... वह ...

जिस ... उसका ...

जहाँ ... वहाँ ...

जब ... तब ...

जैसा ... वैसा ...

जो सत्य है, वही स्थायी है।

These constructions are useful for formal, explanatory, philosophical, and narrative speech.

Do not overuse them simply to make dialogue sound elevated.

---

- register
- character
- emotional state
- sentence complexity
- source influence

Avoid excessive multi-clause sentences in emotionally immediate dialogue.

---

# 33. VOCABULARY REGISTER

The project uses a controlled vocabulary hierarchy.

REGISTER_A:
  ordinary natural Hindi

REGISTER_B:
  refined formal Hindi

REGISTER_C:
  elevated literary Hindi

REGISTER_D:
  Sanskritic terminology

REGISTER_E:
  direct Sanskrit

The highest register is NOT automatically the best register.

Choose the lowest register that accurately communicates the intended dignity and meaning.

---

# 34. SANSKRIT-DERIVED VOCABULARY

Sanskrit-derived Hindi vocabulary is permitted.

Examples:

धर्म
कर्तव्य
मर्यादा
प्रार्थना
आशीर्वाद
वचन
प्रतिज्ञा
करुणा
धैर्य
संकल्प

But Sanskrit-derived vocabulary should remain understandable and natural for the speaker.

Do not turn ordinary dialogue into continuous Sanskrit compounds.

---

# 35. TATSAMA / TADBHAVA CONTROL

The project should recognize the distinction between:

tatsama:
  forms retained relatively close to Sanskrit

tadbhava:
  historically evolved Indo-Aryan forms

The choice must depend on:

- character
- register
- source
- context
- naturalness

Do not choose a tatsama form merely because it sounds more ancient.

---

# 36. COMPOUND WORDS

Long Sanskritic compounds should be used sparingly in ordinary spoken Hindi.

Avoid sentences overloaded with multi-word Sanskrit compounds when a natural Hindi construction communicates the same meaning.

Elevated compounds may be appropriate for:

- formal declarations
- ritual language
- narration
- philosophical statements
- source-derived passages

---

# 37. ENGLISH

English is not the default language of the project.

Avoid unnecessary English words when a natural Hindi equivalent exists.

Forbidden by default:

- okay
- sorry
- guys
- literally
- obviously
- bro
- dude
- cool
- whatever
- etc.

English may appear only when explicitly justified by:

- unavoidable technical production terminology
- metadata
- behind-the-scenes instructions
- subtitle/production workflow
- deliberate source/adaptation decision

It should not enter character dialogue casually.

---

# 38. HINGLISH

Hinglish is NOT the default dialogue language.

Do not use modern mixed Hindi-English patterns merely because they sound conversational.

The project is not attempting to reproduce contemporary urban Indian speech.

---

# 39. SLANG

Avoid:

- contemporary slang
- internet slang
- meme expressions
- Gen-Z expressions
- social-media expressions
- modern insults
- casual profanity

If a source contains a culturally specific harsh expression, preserve or adapt it only after source and context review.

---

# 40. PROFANITY AND OBSCENITY

Default:

  profanity: FORBIDDEN
  obscenity: FORBIDDEN
  sexually vulgar language: FORBIDDEN

Strong emotional speech may still exist without profanity.

Anger should be expressed through:

- word choice
- syntax
- directness
- repetition
- address change
- silence
- rhetorical force

rather than modern abuse.

---

# 41. ROMANTIC LANGUAGE

Romantic dialogue must remain culturally and character appropriate.

Avoid:

- modern dating terminology
- modern relationship slang
- sexually explicit casual language
- social-media romance vocabulary

Love may be expressed through:

- affection
- concern
- respect
- devotion
- longing
- indirect expression
- poetic language
- culturally appropriate address

Do not make every romantic exchange excessively poetic.

---

Sacred terms must be used accurately.

Examples:

तप

ऋषि

Meaning and usage should be checked against the appropriate source/research system.

Do not use sacred terminology merely as decorative vocabulary.

---

# 43. CHARACTER REGISTER

Hindi grammar remains shared across characters.

What changes is primarily:

- vocabulary
- formality
- address
- sentence complexity
- metaphor density
- Sanskrit/Awadhi usage
- directness
- rhetorical style
- emotional restraint

Do not create a separate invented grammar for each character.

---

# 44. ROYAL SPEECH

Royal characters may use:

- controlled formal Hindi
- respectful address
- dignified vocabulary
- concise declarations
- formal requests
- appropriate titles

Avoid making every royal sentence excessively Sanskritized.

Royal speech must still sound speakable.

---

# 45. SAGE / SPIRITUAL SPEECH

Sages may use:

- elevated vocabulary
- philosophical terminology
- source-supported Sanskrit
- concise aphoristic constructions
- formal address

But:

Not every sage must speak entirely in Sanskrit.

Not every sage must speak in poetic metaphors.

Language must follow character and situation.

---

# 46. DIVINE SPEECH

Divine characters may use elevated language when justified.

However:

divine ≠ constant Sanskrit

divine ≠ constant poetry

divine ≠ constant philosophical exposition

Divine speech must remain humanly performable unless the source explicitly requires ritual/mantric language.

---

# 47. EMOTIONAL REGISTER

Hindi wording should change naturally with emotion.

CALM:

- complete sentences
- measured vocabulary
- controlled intensity

ANGER:

- shorter constructions
- greater directness
- stronger address
- possible repetition
- reduced politeness where justified

GRIEF:

- pauses
- incomplete constructions
- repetition
- restrained vocabulary
- silence

DEVOTION:

- respectful address
- devotional terminology
- source-supported expressions

FEAR:

- shorter sentences
- hesitation
- direct questions
- repetition

JOY:

- lighter rhythm
- natural repetition
- exclamatory constructions where appropriate

Do not mechanically apply emotional templates.

---

# 48. DIALOGUE NATURALNESS

A grammatically perfect sentence can still sound unnatural.

Every dialogue line should be checked for:

- speakability
- rhythm
- length
- conversational response
- emotional truth
- redundancy
- unnecessary exposition

Written Hindi must be converted into spoken Hindi when the scene requires spoken performance.

---

# 49. EXPOSITION CONTROL

Characters should not explain information they already know solely for the audience.

Avoid:

"हे भ्राता राम, जैसा कि तुम जानते हो..."

when both characters already know the information.

Information should emerge naturally through:

- action
- conflict
- questioning
- narration
- discovery
- conversation

---

# 50. REPETITION

Repetition may be used for:

- emotion
- emphasis
- ritual
- poetry
- rhetorical force
- source preservation

Avoid accidental repetition caused by AI generation.

---

# 51. METAPHOR

Metaphor is permitted.

However:

Do not make every character poetic.

Do not insert a metaphor into every emotional line.

Metaphor should arise from:

- character
- culture
- situation
- source
- emotion

---

# 52. FILLER WORDS

Modern fillers should not be added automatically.

Avoid unnecessary:

- मतलब
- actually
- basically
- like
- you know
- तो...
- हाँ ना
- यार

Natural hesitation may be represented through:

- pauses
- incomplete sentences
- repetition
- silence

when appropriate.

---

# 53. INTERRUPTION

Interruptions may be used when justified by:

- emotional escalation
- urgency
- disagreement
- fear
- surprise
- natural conversational timing

Do not make every conversation perfectly turn-taking.

---

# 54. SILENCE

Silence is part of dialogue design.

A character does not always need a verbal response.

Use silence when:

- emotion is overwhelming
- respect is appropriate
- an answer is difficult
- a revelation has occurred
- a relationship makes words unnecessary

Silence belongs to performance/script systems but may be indicated in language notes.

---

# 55. PUNCTUATION

Hindi screenplay text should use Devanagari punctuation consistently.

Preferred:

।  पूर्ण विराम

,  अल्पविराम

?  प्रश्नवाचक

!  विस्मयादिबोधक

Quotation marks may be used according to screenplay formatting standards.

Do not mix punctuation systems randomly.

---

# 56. DEVANAGARI

Final Hindi production text should normally use Devanagari.

Roman Hindi is permitted only for:

- transliteration
- pronunciation support
- technical metadata
- research
- subtitle-production workflows where explicitly required

Do not maintain Roman Hindi as the primary screenplay representation.

---

# 57. SPELLING

Use standardized modern Hindi orthography for newly written Hindi unless:

- direct source text is being preserved
- a deliberate historical spelling is being documented
- a textual variant is being recorded

Do not silently modernize source quotations.

Do not introduce inconsistent spellings of character names.

---

# 58. NAMES

Character names must have a controlled canonical spelling.

Each important name should eventually record:

- preferred Hindi spelling
- source spelling
- alternate spellings
- Sanskrit form where relevant
- Awadhi form where relevant
- pronunciation
- approved transliteration

Do not randomly alternate:

राम / रामा / Rama / Raam

within production dialogue.

The approved form must come from the character/language registry.

---

# 59. EPITHETS

Epithets should be used intentionally.

Examples may include:

राम
रघुनाथ
रघुवीर
प्रभु
नरेश

But the exact use must depend on:

- speaker
- listener
- relationship
- context
- source
- emotional state

Do not replace character names with epithets constantly.

---

# 60. DIRECT ADDRESS

Direct address should reflect relationship.

Examples of categories:

- familial
- royal
- devotional
- marital
- hierarchical
- friendly
- formal
- ritual

The exact address term belongs in the relationship profile.

---

# 61. REGISTER TRANSITION

A character may change register within a scene.

Possible causes:

- private vs public setting
- anger
- grief
- authority
- intimacy
- ritual transition
- realization
- social pressure

Such a change should be intentional.

---

# 62. DIALECT POLICY

Do not invent regional dialects for characters without evidence.

Do not assume:

- Ayodhya = exaggerated Awadhi accent
- Mithila = invented Mithila accent
- Lanka = invented Sri Lankan/Hindi accent
- forest characters = rustic Hindi

Geographical identity does not automatically establish a dialogue dialect.

Any dialect decision requires:

- source evidence
- linguistic evidence
- project approval

---

# 63. HISTORICAL LANGUAGE POLICY

The production is not attempting to reproduce a single reconstructed spoken Hindi of an exact historical year.

Therefore:

- do not fabricate historically exact colloquial Hindi
- do not claim modern Standard Hindi is identical to ancient speech
- do not invent period-specific grammar
- do not create fake archaic forms

Instead use:

source-faithful material
+
historically respectful register
+
natural modern performance Hindi

where adaptation requires Hindi.

---

# 64. ACCESSIBILITY

The audience should understand the dialogue without requiring a dictionary for every sentence.

If an uncommon word is necessary:

- context should clarify it
- the word should have a reason for appearing
- the surrounding sentence should remain understandable

Do not simplify everything into ordinary contemporary speech.

---

# 65. PHILOSOPHICAL DIALOGUE

Philosophical language may be sophisticated.

However:

- avoid unnecessary abstraction
- avoid lecture-like dialogue
- avoid repeating the same principle
- avoid giving every character philosophical speech
- preserve source wording where canonical

Complex ideas should be communicated naturally.

---

# 66. FORMALITY SCALE

Use this project scale:

F0:
  intimate / highly familiar

F1:
  familiar

F2:
  neutral respectful

# 67. LANGUAGE DECISION RULE

When choosing between two grammatically correct Hindi expressions:

priority:

1. source fidelity
2. character identity
3. relationship appropriateness
4. cultural appropriateness
5. natural spoken Hindi
6. clarity
7. stylistic elegance

Do not choose an expression merely because it sounds more "mythological."

- terminology review
- cultural review
- continuity review

AI-generated Hindi is not automatically authoritative.
